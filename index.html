Waste Valorization

I'm interested in treating waste as a feedstock rather than something to dispose of, converting CO₂, waste plastics, textile waste, biomass and low-quality used cooking oil into platform chemicals. My work so far has been on plastics, textiles and used cooking oil, and it has shown me how much the right solvent or catalyst can change what is recoverable.

⚙️

Process Intensification

I'm interested in intensified reactors that go beyond conventional fixed-bed designs, such as reactive distillation columns, rotating packed beds, microreactors and spinning disk reactors. More effective heat and mass transfer can mean more product formation, better yields and higher throughput, often from smaller and safer equipment.

The principles behind it:

  • Make every molecular event count, so more collisions lead to the desired reaction
  • Give every molecule the same processing experience
  • Maximize driving forces and interfacial area for heat and mass transfer
  • Combine steps, such as reaction and separation, so they reinforce each other

Novel Intensified Continuous Process for the Synthesis of Paracetamol

Reactive distillationContinuous pilot plant Technology transferred · commercial plant underway in Hyderabad
>98%PAP conversion
90–95%paracetamol yield
99.6%purity (HPLC)
Zeroeffluent discharge

The problem

The industrial batch route suffers from long reaction times, high raw-material consumption per unit of product, reliance on acetic anhydride and the aqueous effluent that comes with it, plus a downstream train of several unit operations to reach pharmaceutical purity.

What we built

  • A continuous, pilot-scale process with a dedicated design for preparing and feeding PAP (para-aminophenol)
  • A multifunctional reactive-distillation reactor that combines reaction and separation
  • Recycling of the acylating agent, cutting raw-material make-up
  • A purification step that uses no organic solvents; product stability matches commercial samples

My contribution

  • Developed the downstream purification, reaching 99.6% purity (HPLC)
  • Ran pilot-plant demonstrations of the continuous process for industrial partners
  • Supported transfer of the technology and process know-how to the licensee

What I learned

Combining reaction and separation in one reactive-distillation unit showed me how much process intensification can simplify a flowsheet. Taking the process from a round-bottom flask to a continuous pilot plant also taught me that purification can be as hard to scale as the reaction itself.

02

Segregation & Depolymerization of Mixed Plastic Waste and Multilayer Plastics (Chips Packets)

Selective solubilizationSolvothermal depolymerization Granted · US Patent 12,679,947 B1
9polymer types separated
>98%BHET purity recovered from chips packets
1US patent granted

The problem

Multilayer packaging stacks different plastics to get strength and barrier properties no single polymer can deliver, which keeps food fresh with less material. But the layers are chemically incompatible, so these films cannot be recycled mechanically and mostly end up in landfill.

What we built

  • A solvent-based route that selectively dissolves and separates nine polymers: PET, PP, PE, PC, PU, PVC, PS, Nylon 6 and Nylon 66
  • For chips packets: shredding → aluminium-layer removal → solvent treatment → separation of PET and PP/PE layers by selective solubilization–precipitation
  • Solvothermal depolymerization of the recovered PET to its monomer, BHET (bis(2-hydroxyethyl) terephthalate)

My contribution

  • Screened solvents across temperatures for solubility, toxicity and cost
  • Applied the selected solvents to depolymerization of the separated plastics, work that forms the basis of US 12,679,947 B1

What I learned

Choosing a solvent turned out to be a balance between solubility, toxicity, cost and temperature rather than a single best answer. Working with real chips packets also showed me how much messier mixed, multilayer waste is than clean, single-polymer plastic.

03

Heterogeneous Catalytic Process for the Synthesis of Bisphenol A (BPA)

Fixed-bed reactorLab → pilot scale-up Pilot-scale process · WO2024189654A1
85%BPA yield
90–95%selectivity
99.5%purity (HPLC)
Reusablecatalyst, no loss in activity

Why it matters

BPA is the building block of polycarbonate (bottles, lenses, electronics) and epoxy resins (construction, automotive, aerospace, coatings), and India relies heavily on imports to meet its demand.

What we built

  • An in-house heterogeneous catalytic route, optimized for temperature, pressure, mole ratio and catalyst loading in 0.45–5 L high-pressure reactors
  • A continuous pilot plant at CSIR-NCL to study feed flow rate, catalyst loading and the fixed-bed temperature profile on conversion, selectivity and yield
  • A downstream protocol giving white crystalline BPA whose physicochemical properties match commercial samples, now protected under WO2024189654A1

My contribution

  • Optimized reaction conditions to a maximum yield of 85%
  • Developed the purification process delivering 99.5% pure BPA
  • Scaled the process from batch to pilot scale

What I learned

Moving from 0.45 L batch reactors to a continuous fixed-bed pilot plant showed me that conditions that work in batch need rethinking at scale, especially the feed flow rate and the temperature profile along the bed. It also made me curious about reactor designs with better heat and mass transfer than a fixed bed.
